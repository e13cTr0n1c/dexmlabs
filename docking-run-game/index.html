Docking Run

Fly a crew capsule onto a space station's docking port, by hand. Twenty thrusters at their real positions, a rigid body that keeps turning once it starts, and orbital mechanics that make every burn do something slightly unexpected.

Touch the ring at 3 to 10 centimetres a second, within 10 cm of the centreline and 4° of alignment, and the latches catch you. Any faster and you bounce or break something.

How to fly a docking


You start on the V-bar, the line through the station along its orbit, with the station's docking port facing you. Point your nose down the port's axis, get onto the centreline, and close slowly. The docking camera looks along your docking axis from a short boom, with a fixed reticle. The station has a target on a standoff: when the white cross sits in the middle of the ring behind it, you're lined up both in position and in angle. If the cross looks off to one side of the ring you are tilted, not just offset. The lime diamond marks the cross so you can find it from far out and at night.


Translate and rotate are separate, as on real spacecraft. W, S, A, D, R and F slide the capsule along its own axes without turning it, as long as you aren't turned already. I, K, J and L, or the arrow keys, pitch and yaw. U and O, or Q and E, roll. Nothing in space stops a rotation: tap a key and the capsule keeps turning until you tap the other way. Kill rotation (H) fires the thrusters for you to hold your attitude relative to the station whenever you aren't commanding a turn. It's honest about propellant.


The physics


The capsule is a rigid body of about 7 tonnes, with a full inertia tensor, including small products of inertia, and a centre of mass a few centimetres off its axis. Its twenty thrusters sit in two rings at real positions on the hull, each with a direction. Every thrust makes a force and a torque about the centre of mass, so a pure translation burn also turns you slightly, and so does a long one. Rotation follows Euler's equations, so a spin about an awkward axis wobbles while its angular momentum stays fixed. The primary jets give 130 newtons and the fine vernier jets 26, burning propellant at a specific impulse of 290 seconds.


Relative motion near the station follows the Clohessy-Wiltshire, or Hill, equations for a circular orbit at 400 km, where one orbit takes about 92 minutes (n ≈ 0.00113 rad/s). They are why docking feels backwards. Burn toward a station ahead of you and the Coriolis term bends your path downward. Burn up, away from Earth, and you end up behind. A point on the V-bar is a free parking spot, while 30 m below the station you slowly drift forward and fall away. The Hill plot in the corner shows the next 45 minutes if you stopped thrusting. The station flies in local vertical, local horizontal hold, so it turns once an orbit, as do the sun, the stars and the ground beneath you.


The five runs


Final approach starts 30 m out, a little off the axis and crooked. 200 m approach starts further out and drifting, so the orbital mechanics shows. Spinning station has lost attitude control and spins at 1°/s about its long axis with a slow wobble: match its roll, then fly into a port that keeps moving. Far rendezvous starts 2 km ahead and 120 m above the station on a slightly higher, slower orbit, drifting back toward it. Use time warp, up to 100× while you're far out, and come down onto the axis. Night pass happens in Earth's shadow, with only the station's lights, your floodlight and the numbers.

Docking limits and scoring
There are two difficulties. Pilot, the default, uses the same physics with a forgiving capture ring (up to 0.25 m/s, 0.3 m off the centreline and 10° out), 60% more propellant, kill rotation switched on, and the orbital drift turned down on the first three runs. It also draws a glowing approach corridor, a closing speed gauge and arrows for the keys to press. Realistic uses the real limits below and full orbital mechanics everywhere. The Daily Drop is always flown in Pilot. Start with First docking: ten metres out, lined up, just drift in.


At first contact with the soft capture ring you need a closing rate between 0.03 and 0.1 m/s, less than 0.1 m off the centreline, less than 4° of misalignment and roll, rates under 0.4°/s and sideways sliding under 0.04 m/s. Then the latches catch, the ring damps out what's left, retracts and pulls you in, and twelve hooks close for hard capture. Too slow and the latches don't trip, slightly off and the ring's springs push you back out, too hard and you've damaged the port.


A docking scores up to 250 for closing rate (best at 0.065 m/s), 250 for centreline, 200 for alignment and roll, 100 for rates, 250 for propellant left and 200 for time, less 100 for every bounce. Your best for each run is kept in this browser.

Questions


How fast do spacecraft dock?
Slowly. Modern docking systems want a closing speed of a few centimetres a second, around 0.03 to 0.1 m/s, with the two ports within about 10 cm of each other and a few degrees of alignment. Docking Run uses those limits.
Why does thrusting toward the station make you drift down?
Near a station in orbit you are both falling around Earth. Speed up along your orbit and you climb into a higher, slower orbit and fall behind; slow down and you drop lower and pull ahead. These are the Clohessy-Wiltshire, or Hill, equations, and they make closing along the orbit (the V-bar) bend your path.
What are soft capture and hard capture?
Soft capture is the first gentle latch: a ring on springs and dampers grabs the arriving vehicle and soaks up its leftover motion. The ring then retracts and pulls the two together, and a ring of hooks drives closed to make an airtight hard capture.
Why does my capsule start turning when I only translate?
The thrusters are at real positions on the hull and the centre of mass is not exactly on the axis, so a translation burn also makes a small torque. In space nothing stops a rotation once it starts. Fly it out, or switch on kill rotation.
What is fine mode?
Small vernier thrusters, 26 newtons each instead of 130. They waste less propellant and let you set a closing rate to the millimetre per second. Real crews use them for the last metres.
Is this a real spacecraft?
No. The capsule and station are original designs with realistic masses, thrust and docking limits. The orbital mechanics and rigid body physics are real. It isn't a training tool, and it has no connection with any space agency.
