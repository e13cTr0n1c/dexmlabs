WORK THE WORLD.

A practical guide to your first operating day, and the radio ideas behind every opening.

Skywave is a game. Its propagation model is simplified and is not a prediction tool. For real conditions use VOACAP or live propagation data.

How to play

Select PLAY DAILY for the same UTC-seeded puzzle as everyone else, or PRACTICE to experiment with solar flux and K. Read the briefing, find a station with an open listening window, tune your radio and press CALL. Work each of the ten targets once, before the day ends.

Target

Pick a fictional station from the list, the globe pin, or the target selector. Its location, locator, rarity, listening window, bands and modes are shown before you transmit.

Band, mode and power

Choose from eleven bands, three modes and 5, 50, 100 or 400 W. Tuning costs no game time, but unsupported antenna-band combinations are disabled with an explanation.

Antenna and bearing

Pick an antenna pattern suited to the distance. The bearing control appears only for the Yagi.

UTC clock

Use +10m, +1h, the slider or WAIT FOR WINDOW. Time cannot move backwards, and a call starting at 23:50 ends the day at 24:00.

CALL

A call costs ten minutes even when the station is off-air or the path fails. A successful target is logged once; failure diagnostics explain what stopped the signal.

Intelligence

Reveal MUF or D-layer loss for a 5% penalty each, or a six-hour rough MUF scope for 10%. These penalties apply once each to that target only and add together; previously worked targets cannot be charged.

Map, log and settings

Drag the globe to orbit and use CENTRE DX or HOME QTH to recenter. Expand the text log and hop diagnostics to review attempts; Settings controls quality, units, layers, sound and reduced motion.

Finish and share

Work all ten targets, reach 24:00, or end the day yourself. Results show paths, score, best DX and a missed-target tip, with clipboard and native-share options where the browser allows them.

Keyboard and touch

Use 1-9, 0 and - for the bands in display order; C calls. Right arrow advances ten minutes and up arrow advances one hour. Shortcuts do not override form controls, open dialogs or modifier-key combinations. On a phone, tap RADIO DESK to expand the bottom sheet and scroll its controls independently.

Scoring

Base points are the rounded distance in kilometres divided by 100, multiplied by rarity, mode and power. Mode multipliers are FT8 1.0, CW 1.5 and SSB 2.0; power multipliers are 5 W 3.0, 50 W 1.5, 100 W 1.0 and 400 W 0.7. A grey-line contact adds 20%, then purchased hint penalties reduce that contact only. Distance units in Settings change display, not the scoring calculation.

Each confirmed QSO shows the sum on a card, for example 113 × 4 × 1.5 × 3 = 2,034 for an 11,347 km CW contact at 5 W with a rarity 4 station. Intel shows as a minus percentage, such as − 10% intel. A comet runs along the path to the station and your score counts up. With reduced motion on, in Settings or on your device, you get the card and the new score straight away.

Five starter tips

  1. Try low bands after dark; explore higher bands by day when SFI is high.
  2. For a station a few hundred kilometres away, try a low dipole and 40m.
  3. When the signal is above MUF, lower the frequency. More power cannot fix an escaping hop.
  4. Start with FT8 and 100 W to learn a route, then try less power or a higher-scoring mode on other targets.
  5. Prioritize windows that close early. Waiting is useful, but you cannot recover elapsed time.

The propagation handbook

Skywave

Your signal leaves the Earth, refracts through an ionospheric layer and returns to the ground. Long routes may need several hops, with a ground reflection between them. Skywave models the short great-circle route rather than the long way around.

The F2 layer

In the game, the F2 layer sits 300 km above the Earth. Its critical frequency rises with sunlight and solar flux, and falls during geomagnetic storms. The layer is drawn eight times higher than scale so you can see the geometry.

The D layer

The lower D layer absorbs energy rather than bringing your signal home. It is strongest in sunlight and especially punishing at low frequencies. Every skywave hop crosses it twice; a scheduled flare multiplies sunlit loss by five.

MUF

The maximum usable frequency is the upper frequency limit for a particular path. Each hop has its own limit, based on its midpoint and hop length. The lowest hop MUF controls the whole route; above it, the signal escapes.

LUF

The lowest usable frequency is the practical lower edge of an opening. Skywave does not assign one fixed LUF number: it tests whether absorption and noise leave enough signal to decode. More power or a more sensitive mode can help at the lower edge, but not above the MUF.

Skip distance and the skip zone

A skywave signal may return to Earth beyond a nearby station rather than at it. Ground wave covers only a short distance in this game. When a short route is beyond ground-wave range and cannot work by NVIS, it is in the skip zone.

NVIS

Near-vertical incidence skywave sends energy steeply upward to reach nearby stations. In Skywave, routes under 500 km need a frequency below the overhead F2 critical frequency and an antenna with high-angle energy. A low dipole is a good starting point; a low-angle vertical is not.

The grey line

The grey line is the day-night boundary around the Earth. In this game, both ends must have the sun between 6 degrees below and 6 degrees above the horizon. On 160m through 30m, this gives 4 dB of signal enhancement and a 20% scoring bonus on a successful contact.

Solar flux / SFI

SFI is the daily solar-flux input to the simplified model. Higher values raise the F2 critical frequency, often opening higher bands. It is not a promise that every high-band path is open.

K and A indices

K describes the geomagnetic activity used for this game day, from 0 to 7. A is a consistent daily indicator derived from K, not another independent penalty. High K depresses the modeled F2 layer, raises noise and adds loss on polar paths.

Sporadic E

During northern-summer practice or daily dates, up to three localized E-layer patches may appear. A 500-2200 km single-hop route on 10m or 6m can open if its midpoint lies inside an active, sunlit patch. The briefing lists the patch centres, radii and UTC windows.

Take-off angle

Take-off angle is the elevation at which your signal leaves the antenna. Longer hops normally use lower angles; nearby skywave contacts need steep angles. The geometry uses a 300 km F2 height and a 3-degree minimum, giving a maximum hop of about 3225 km in this implementation.

Choose your antenna

A low dipole favours NVIS, while the half-wave-high dipole balances medium-angle routes. A vertical favours low angles; the EFHW has a simplified, slightly reduced dipole-like pattern. The magnetic loop trades gain for lower local noise, and the Yagi adds directional gain only on its supported bands.

Aim the Yagi

The Yagi supports 20m through 10m, including 17m and 12m. Use the bearing control to aim toward the target, or press AIM AT DX BEARING. An off-beam signal loses gain; the modeled rear direction is 20 dB down from the front.

FT8

FT8 is the most sensitive game mode, decoding at -20 dB SNR. It scores a mode multiplier of 1.0. The generated warble is a sound effect, not a valid encoded FT8 transmission.

CW

CW uses Morse-like tone bursts and decodes at -10 dB SNR in Skywave. Its mode multiplier is 1.5. CW is a useful middle ground between FT8 sensitivity and SSB scoring.

SSB

SSB is the least sensitive game mode, requiring +6 dB SNR. It earns a 2.0 mode multiplier but is not available on 30m. The synthetic radio noise is an effect, not recorded speech.

QSB and decode margin

A signal only 0-3 dB above its decode threshold is marginal. Each marginal call has a deterministic, seeded 50% chance of decoding. Both success and failure show QSB, so a narrow failure can be a fading event rather than a fundamentally closed path.

Beyond the game

Use VOACAP Online for real propagation planning, and DX cluster maps for observed activity. Skywave does not check local allocations, licensing, station permission or interference, and its frequencies are game inputs rather than operating advice.
