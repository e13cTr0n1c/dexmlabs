W and S, or up and down, step the throttle 5% at a time; it lights at 40% and goes off below that. Space is full thrust and X cuts the engine. How a booster comes home


After it has pushed the upper stage on its way, the first stage of a reusable rocket turns round and falls back, engines first. It has very little fuel left, so almost all the slowing down is done by the air and by one short, late, hard burn. Out at sea a flat ship holds station with its own thrusters and waits.


In the game the booster weighs about 20 tonnes empty and carries a few tonnes of fuel. Its engines give 440 kN together, about twice its weight, and burn around 150 kg a second at full thrust. It throttles from 40% to 100%. At 40% it is a little weaker than gravity, so you can come down gently, and around 55% it hovers, though that eats fuel fast. The air does the rest: falling through thick air the booster settles at about 140 m/s, faster higher up where the air is thin.


The red BURN mark on the height tape shows where a full thrust burn started now would stop you, ignoring the air. Light the engine a little above it, then ease off as you slow. Hovering all the way down works, but it costs most of your fuel, and fuel left is points.


Steering: lean, glide and gimbal


Q and E fire small cold gas thrusters at the top to lean the booster. Let go and the flight computer holds the angle, just as it does in the Apollo game. With the engine burning, a lean turns some of the thrust sideways, which is how you cover big distances. With the engine off, the grid fins at the top let you glide: lean the way you want to go and the air pushes you there.


A and D swing the engine a few degrees on its gimbal. In the game the computer keeps the booster upright while you do it, so it works as a fine sideways push for the last few metres. It only works while the engine is lit.


Wind, swell, heat and sound


Wind pushes the long body sideways, more strongly higher up, and it gusts. The deck heaves up and down and pitches end to end with the swell, so the touchdown speed that counts is your speed relative to the deck. A deck rising into you lands harder. The deck readout says whether it is rising or falling.


On the night and storm levels you start supersonic. Coming through thick air that fast heats the engine section, so you light the engine for a few seconds high up. That is the entry burn: it slows you, and the plume pushes the hot shock away from the engines. Slow through Mach 1 and you leave a sonic boom behind you. Legs come out only below 90 m/s, because the airflow would tear them off.


Tower catch


The big stainless booster has no legs. It flies back to the launch site and the tower catches it: two arms on a carriage swing shut under a pair of catch pins just below the grid fins. Come in beside the tower, on the right of it, never over it. Slow to a hover with the pins level with the arms, centred between them, and press C. The arms take most of a second to close, so you need to be steady when they arrive: within 3 m up or down and 3 m of centre, drifting under 2 m/s, coming down under 2.5 m/s and tilted less than 6°. Miss and they open again for another try, if you have the fuel. Close them on the side of the booster and it's over.


With arm assist on, the carriage rides up and down to meet your pins and the tower closes the arms the moment you are in the window. It is much easier and scores ×0.75. The panel on the left of the picture shows each part of the window going green.


Scoring


A landing scores 200, plus up to 300 for a soft touchdown, 150 for landing level with the deck, 100 for not sliding, 350 for landing near the centre of the ring, 250 for fuel left and 150 for a bullseye within 3 m. Windy pays ×1.5, Night ×2 and Storm ×3. Catches score the same way against the catch window: calm ×1.5, crosswind ×2.5 and night ×3.5. Your best on each level is kept on this device. After any flight, V plays back the last five seconds at half speed.

Questions


Is this a real rocket?

How does a tower catch work?
The booster has no legs. It hovers beside the launch tower with its catch pins level with two arms on a carriage, and the arms swing shut under the pins and take its weight. In the game you close them with C, or let arm assist do it.
Why does a booster land on a ship and not back on land?
Flying all the way back costs fuel that a heavy launch cannot spare, so the booster carries on downrange and a ship waits for it out at sea.
What is an entry burn?
A short burn high up, before the thick air. It slows the booster and its plume pushes the hot shock layer away from the engines. In the game it keeps the heat gauge out of the red on the night and storm levels.
Why do the legs only come out below 90 m/s?
In the game the legs tear off in a fast airflow, so a lock stops you deploying them until you have slowed down. Deploy them, then keep it slow.
What are grid fins for?
They are the lattice fins at the top. Falling base first through the air, they let the booster lean and glide sideways without using the engine, and they keep it pointing the right way.
Can it hover?
In the game, yes, just. At minimum throttle the engine is a little weaker than the weight, so you can come down slowly, and around 55% it holds you still. Real boosters are often too strong to hover and have to time it perfectly.
