The physics of a lunar landing

The Moon pulls at 1.62 m/s², a sixth of Earth's, and there's no air, so nothing slows you but the engine. The lunar module's descent engine gave about 45,000 newtons and could be throttled between about 10% and 65%. Burning propellant at a specific impulse of 311 seconds, it used roughly 14.8 kg a second at full thrust, less as you throttle down. As fuel burns off the craft gets lighter, so the same throttle slows you more.

To hover, thrust must equal weight. With about 7,600 kg of lunar module that's close to 27% throttle, which is why a minute or two of hover time was all the crews had left in the last phase. Touch down too hard or sideways and the landing gear, built for about 3 m/s down and 1.2 m/s across, gives way.

The real landings

Six Apollo crews landed between July 1969 and December 1972. On Apollo 11, Neil Armstrong took manual control to fly past a boulder field, with computer alarms going off, and Mission Control called 30 seconds of fuel shortly before touchdown in the Sea of Tranquillity. Later crews landed in the Ocean of Storms, at Fra Mauro, beside Hadley Rille, in the Descartes highlands and in the Taurus Littrow valley.
